# Troyes, July 1250 — what is evidenced, what is reasoned, what is invented

This file exists so that nothing in the picture has to be taken on trust. Every
building in the render falls into one of three classes, and they are marked
throughout as **[A]**, **[B]** or **[C]**:

- **[A] Evidenced.** The building existed on that site in 1250, and the site is
  still identifiable on the ground today.
- **[B] Reasoned.** The building existed, but its position, size or appearance
  is inferred — from what replaced it, from what survives elsewhere in
  Champagne, or from what the documents imply without stating.
- **[C] Invented.** Ordinary fabric with no individual evidence behind it: the
  three thousand houses, the field pattern, the stalls, the crowd. These are
  built to type, not to record.

---

## 1. The date, and why it is a good one

**24 June–end of July 1250.** The Hot Fair of Saint-Jean opened on 24 June and
ran for weeks. The render is set on a July morning about two hours after
sunrise, with the fair in full swing.

What is happening elsewhere that summer, for context:

- **Thibaut IV** — Thibaut le Chansonnier, Count of Champagne and, since 1234,
  King of Navarre — is the lord of this town. He has about three years to live
  (d. 8 July 1253). The fairs are his family's great creation and his revenue.
- **Louis IX is a prisoner in Egypt.** The king was taken at Fariskur in April
  1250 after the disaster at Mansurah; his ransom is being raised through the
  spring and summer. News of it would have reached the fair.
- **The Champagne fairs are at their absolute peak.** Within two generations
  they will be finished, killed by the direct sea route from Italy to Bruges
  and by the county's absorption into the royal domain after Jeanne of Navarre
  married Philip the Fair in 1284.

## 2. The three things most reconstructions of Troyes get wrong

### 2.1 Saint-Urbain is not there **[A, by absence]**

The Basilique Saint-Urbain is one of the finest things in Troyes and it cannot
appear in a view of 1250. Jacques Pantaléon, son of a Troyes cobbler, was
elected pope as **Urban IV in 1261**. In **1262** he founded a collegiate
church on the exact site of his father's shop, with Jean Langlois as architect;
choir and transept went up fast, 1262–66.

In 1250 that site holds a shop in a street, and Jacques Pantaléon is abroad in
church service. The render puts **an ordinary timber house on the spot** —
which is, as far as the evidence goes, exactly what was there.

### 2.2 The cathedral is a building site **[A/B]**

Saint-Pierre-et-Saint-Paul in July 1250:

| Element | State | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Chevet, ambulatory, radiating chapels | Complete. Begun c. 1200 under Bp Garnier de Traînel, working from the chapels on the **north** side of the ambulatory first | **[A]** |
| Choir upper walls and glazed triforium | Complete and only ten years old. The 1228 cyclone threw down the upper works and the lower part of the choir's **south** aisle; rebuilt **1235–40**, and the rebuilders glazed the triforium — an idea barely tried before, at Saint-Denis | **[A]** |
| Transept | Walls part-risen, **no vault**. It is not vaulted until c. 1310 | **[A]** for the date, **[B]** for how far the walls had got |
| Nave | Does not exist. 14th–15th century | **[A]** |
| West front, towers | Do not exist | **[A]** |
| The older nave still standing west of the crossing | **[B]** — see below |
| Cranes, scaffolding, lodge, stone yard, lime pits | **[C]**, built to type |

The weakest link is the **west end**. The old cathedral burned in 1188. The
chapter still had to sing the office somewhere through fifty years of building,
and the normal medieval practice was to keep the old nave in use behind a
temporary wall while the new choir went up east of it. That is what is modelled:
a lower, patched Romanesque nave with a bell-cote, shut off from the new work
by a stop wall. It is a reasoned guess, not a record.

The height difference is the point of the image. The new choir stands about
29.5 m to the vault and near 48 m to the ridge; the old nave beside it is barely
16 m. That step is what a Gothic building campaign actually looked like from
the air.

### 2.3 The half-timbering is the wrong half-timbering **[B]**

This matters, because it is the thing Troyes is famous for.

The Ruelle des Chats, the Rue Champeaux frontages, the Hôtel de Vauluisant,
the tall carved jettied frames in every photograph of Troyes — **those are the
rebuilding after the fire of 1524**, not medieval fabric.

Worse, there had already been a fire: on **23 July 1188, during the fair
itself**, most of the town burned, taking the cathedral, the comital palace,
the collegiate church of Saint-Étienne and the abbey of Notre-Dame-aux-Nonnains
with it. Reconstruction began around 1200.

So the houses in this render are **all roughly one generation old** — built
since about 1195 — and they are modelled accordingly:

- one or two storeys and an attic, not three and four;
- a shallow jetty of 0.3 m or so, not the deep oversailing of the 1500s;
- plain straight braces, no carving, no ornamental lozenges;
- **oak left bare and weathered grey-brown.** The black-painted frames are a
  much later fashion, in places a 19th- and 20th-century one;
- steep roofs, gable to the street on the burgage plots;
- **flat clay tile** in the fair quarter and on the main streets, **thatch and
  oak shingle** surviving on the back lanes — the count had every reason to push
  tile after 1188, but it has not driven thatch out yet;
- a handful of **stone** houses for the richest merchants, as at Provins;
- **chimneys on about one house in eight.** A flue is still a novelty and a mark
  of money in 1250; most of these houses vent through a roof louvre.

### The comital complex — on the Cité/Bourg boundary

demolished at the Revolution and the palace site became a timber port when the

### The Jewish quarter **[B]**

Troyes was Rashi's town — Rabbi Shlomo Yitzhaki died there in 1105 — and the
community was still present in 1250, though under increasing pressure: the
Talmud was burned in Paris in 1242, and the general expulsion from France comes
in 1306.

Two quarters are attested. The older, in the **Saint-Frobert** district around
**rue Hennequin**, was nicknamed *la Juiverie* or the **Brosse aux Juifs**;
**rue du Paon** is said to have held Rashi's own house. A second quarter grows
up from the 12th or 13th century around **rue de la Synagogue**, on the north
flank of Saint-Pantaléon. Both streets are laid out in the render; nothing in
the built fabric distinguishes them, which is honest — there is no evidence that
the houses looked different.

## 4. Water **[A in outline, B in detail]**

The most under-appreciated thing about medieval Troyes. From the second half of
the 12th century the counts canalised the Seine to drain the marsh, win building
land, drive mills and flood the ditches. South of the town the river divides
into four:

- **canal des Trévois** — attributed to Henri le Libéral
- **Ru Cordé**
- **canal de la Moline**, also called the **Planche-Clément** — mentioned as
  early as **1134**
- **la Pielle**

Downstream the Planche-Clément divides again into the **Meldançon**, running
north, and the **canal de Jaillard**, which drives the Jaillard mill, attested
in **1152**. All of these are in the render, with mills on them. Their exact
courses in 1250 are **[B]**: the modern Canal de la Haute-Seine, cut on
Napoleon's orders, has overwritten the central one.

Mills on the *rus* drove not only corn but the drapers' fulling stocks and the
tanners' bark mills — which is why the **rue des Anciennes Tanneries** sits
where it does, downstream on the Ru Cordé.

## 5. The walls **[B — the weakest part of the reconstruction]**

**The champagne-cork outline is not the 1250 circuit.** This is the single
easiest mistake to make, because the cork is how everyone pictures Troyes.

The counts pushed the ramparts outward through the 12th and 13th centuries, and
the line only reached its **greatest extent at the end of the 13th century**.
The wall that the 19th-century boulevards replaced was later still — rebuilt and
repaired down to the 17th century, by then 5.2 km round with 21 gates.

What is drawn is the **mid-century state**: the castrum curtain around the Cité,
and a comital wall with a wet ditch around the Bourg, already roughly
cork-shaped but tighter than the final line. Treat the wall line as **±50 m**
and the gate positions as indicative. The gate names used — Madeleine, Croncels,
Saint-Jacques, Preize, Chaillouet, Comtale, Girouarde — are attested Troyes gate
names, but not all of them are securely attested *for 1250 in these positions*.

## 6. Streets

Names marked **(M)** below are attested medieval names the street still carries
today, and they are the ones a merchant at the fair would have used:

- **rue Champeaux (M)**
- **rue de la Montée des Changes (M)** — the money-changers' street. The
  *changeurs* were mostly Italians acting for the great houses at home
- **rue de la Pierre (M)**
- **rue des Anciennes Tanneries (M)**
- **ruelle des Chats (M)** — the famous alley, though the houses that now nearly
  meet across it are post-1524
- **rue de la Cité** — the Roman *decumanus maximus*
- **rue de la Tour / rue Boucherat** — the Roman *cardo maximus*

The rest of the street names in the model are ordinary topographic names of the
quarter (grand rue du Bourg, rue de Croncels, rue Saint-Pantaléon and so on) and
are **[B]/[C]**: plausible, not documented in that form for 1250.

## 7. The fair itself **[A in structure, C in detail]**

Six great fairs ran through the year in the county — two at Provins, one at
Lagny, one at Bar-sur-Aube, and the two big ones at Troyes: the **hot** fair of
Saint-Jean from 24 June and the **cold** fair of Saint-Rémi from 1 October.

The counts supplied the plant — halls, scales, weights and measures, closely
policed — and the ***Conduit des Foires***, the safe-conduct that protected a
merchant and his goods on the road even beyond the county's borders. That legal
guarantee, more than geography, is why the fairs worked.

The trade: cloth, wool and furs out of Flanders and the north, exchanged against
silk, spices and alum from the Levant by way of Genoa and Venice. Payment
through the *changeurs*. The pitches in the render — drapery, mercery, old
clothes, poultry, grain, salt, furs, money — are specialised by street and
square as the sources describe, but the individual stalls are **[C]**.

The camps outside the gates are **[B]**: the walls demonstrably could not hold
the fair, and tented lodging, horse lines and waggon parks outside the gates are
what that implies.

## 8. Landscape **[B/C]**

- **Troyes is flat.** There is no hill near it and no natural ground from which
  to look down on the town. The plain moves barely a metre across the whole
  frame. This is why the cathedral and the abbey towers carry the skyline alone,
  and it is the most important single fact about the site.
- **Open-field country**, ploughed in long narrow lands gathered into furlongs,
  the direction turning from one furlong to the next, with grass headlands
  between. Late July: wheat and barley standing or just cut, a third of the land
  in fallow under the three-field rota, hay meadow along the water.
- **No Lombardy poplars.** *Populus nigra* 'Italica' does not reach western
  Europe until the middle of the 18th century. The trees here are oak, elm, ash
  and field maple on the headlands, willow and alder on the wet ground — all
  round-crowned, none conical.

## 9. Light

The sun position is computed rather than guessed. For Troyes (48.30° N, 4.08° E)
with a solar declination of about **+19.8°** on 24 July:

> sin(alt) = sin(48.3°)·sin(19.8°) + cos(48.3°)·cos(19.8°)·cos(H)

gives an altitude of **20°** at an hour angle near 82° — roughly two hours after
sunrise, about the hour the stalls open. At that altitude the azimuth works out
at **82°**, a little north of due east, and every roof throws a shadow about 2.7
times its own height towards the west-south-west. That is the light in the
render.

## 10. Known limits

Stated plainly, so the picture is not trusted further than it deserves:

1. **No map access.** This was built without the ability to reach a mapping
   service or fetch a plan — the network here allows search but not page
   retrieval. The georeferencing in `scene/js/survey.js` is derived from
   documented site relationships plus knowledge of the modern street plan, not
   from measuring a map. Expect **±20 m on the churches** and **±50 m on the
   wall lines**. The relative arrangement — which building is north or east of
   which, and which way each faces — is the part worth trusting.
2. **The cathedral axis** is taken as **85°** (apse 5° north of true east), and
   the whole Roman grid of the Cité with it. Gothic churches are rarely oriented
   exactly, and this figure is an adopted value, not a survey.
3. **The castrum**, at 400 m square, is placed so that its west curtain falls
   between the cathedral and the comital palace, because Saint-Étienne is
   documented as built *outside* it. That constraint fixes the wall within
   perhaps 50 m; nothing sharper.
4. **Populations.** Estimates for Troyes at the start of the 13th century run
   from 15,000 to 30,000 and swelled hugely during a fair. The render carries
   about 3,300 houses and a visible crowd of a few thousand; it is not a census.
5. **Interiors, and anything below eye level,** are not modelled at all.
6. **No documentary evidence was fetched in full text.** Findings came through
   search result summaries; primary sources and the scholarly literature
   (Boutiot, Roserot, the *Cahiers de civilisation médiévale* work on
   Saint-Étienne, the regional archaeological surveys) were not read directly.
   Anything here would be worth checking against them before being relied on.

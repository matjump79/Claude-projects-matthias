# Ulm in 1500 — what the film shows, and on what grounds

This file separates three things that reconstructions usually blur together:
what is **documented**, what is **inferred** from the documented state, and
what is **invented** because no evidence survives. Read it as the film's
apparatus criticus.

---

## 1. The date matters: 1500 is not "the old town"

The Ulm that visitors photograph today is largely the Ulm of the 19th century
and of the rebuilding after 17 December 1944. Four things that everyone
associates with the city are **deliberately absent** from this film, because
they did not exist in 1500:

| Not shown | Why |
|---|---|
| The Minster's 161.5 m spire | Finished **1890**. In 1500 the tower was a square stump with a temporary roof. |
| The painted Rathaus façade | Painted between about **1520 and 1540**. |
| The Rathaus astronomical clock | Installed about **1520**. |
| A five-aisled Minster nave | Burkhard Engelberg's rebuilding runs **1502–1507**. In 1500 the church is still a three-aisled basilica. |

Also absent: the Neue Straße (driven through the ruins after 1945), the
Schwörhaus in its present form (1613), the Neuer Bau (1585–93), the Kornhaus
(1594), and Neu-Ulm on the far bank (founded 1810 — in 1500 that side is field,
meadow and the road to Bavaria).

## 2. The Minster — the centrepiece, and a building site

Documented, and modelled accordingly:

- **Foundation stone 1377.** It is a *parish* church, built and paid for by the
  townspeople, not a bishop's cathedral. Ulm never had a bishop; it belonged to
  the diocese of Constance.
- **Matthäus Böblinger** took over in 1477 and revised the west tower design.
  The tower reached the **square platform at about 70 m** — the stage King
  **Maximilian I climbed on his visit in July 1492**.
- **1492: stones fell from the vault** of the under-founded tower during a
  midday sermon and the congregation fled. Work on the tower stopped.
- The square was **closed in 1494**; the octagon Böblinger had begun above it
  was **broken off after about five metres** and covered with a **pyramidal
  emergency roof**. That roof stayed until the tower was completed in 1890.
- **Burkhard Engelberg** became master mason in **1493** and is credited with
  saving the building; his rebuilding of the aisles (three naves into five) runs
  **1502–1507** — i.e. *after* our date.
- Finished dimensions used for proportion: length **123.56 m**, width 48.8 m
  (five-aisled), central nave **41.6 m**, aisles 20.55 m.

Inferred (reasonable, not documented in this detail):

- The **choir flanking towers** appear as low shafts with provisional roofs.
  Their present 86 m form is 19th-century. That they stood unfinished in 1500 is
  secure; their exact height then is not, and the film picks a plausible one.
- **Scaffolding and a treadwheel crane** on the tower. The lodge (*Bauhütte*)
  was demonstrably active — Engelberg was reinforcing the piers — so building
  plant is right in kind; its exact placement is invented.
- Window tracery is painted onto the wall surfaces rather than modelled. The
  *rhythm* of the openings is right; the patterns are generic late Gothic.

## 3. The fortifications

- The circuit is **brick**, roughly **3,500 m** long and about **9 m** high,
  begun from 1316.
- **Six main gates**: Herdbrucker Tor (demolished 1827), Göcklertor (1837),
  Frauentor (1837), Neutor (1860), plus the gates that survive — the
  **Gänstor** and the **Seelturm/Zundeltor** — and the **Metzgerturm**.
- **1480: the southern wall was pushed down to the Danube** and stands directly
  in the water; iron rings for mooring boats are still in it. This is exactly
  what the earliest picture of the city shows, and the film reproduces it.
- The **Metzgerturm**, built about 1340 as an outlet of the Staufen-era
  fortification towards the slaughterhouse: **c. 36 m**, out of plumb by
  **2.05 m** (3.3°) to the north-west. The film models the lean.
- The **Adlerbastei** on the river front dates from about 1480.

Inferred: the exact number, spacing and shape of the intermediate wall towers;
the landward ditch and low outer wall (normal practice, and present in later
views); the gate towers' individual profiles.

## 4. The plan of the town

The layout is **proportionate, not surveyed**. The relationships that are
right:

- The town sits on the **north** bank of the Danube; the Danube runs west to
  east past its southern wall.
- The **Blau** comes out of the Alb from the west, splits, drives the mills of
  the fishermen's and tanners' quarter, and joins the Danube **inside** the
  walls.
- The **Herdbrucke** is the only bridge, leading south out of the Herdbrucker
  gate — "the sole access from the south", as the 1493 view shows it.
- The **Minster stands free** in the middle of the town, not on the market. The
  **Rathaus** lies roughly 200 m south of it, the **Weinhof** — the old royal
  hall, the city's origin — south-west of that.
- Distances: Minster to Danube c. 430 m, walled area c. 85 ha.

The street lines themselves are **invented**, laid out in the radial-concentric
pattern normal for a town of this kind: lanes running from each gate towards the
market, a lane inside the wall, dense frontages with yards behind.

## 5. Buildings and their look

- **Burgher houses**: narrow plots with the **gable to the street**, two to four
  storeys, masonry or heavy timber ground floor with the trade in it, framed and
  lime-washed floors above, often **jettied** over the lane, steep **tiled**
  roofs with a hoist loft. Tile, not thatch: south German councils pushed tile
  against fire risk from the 14th century.
- **Timbers are weathered oak**, not black. The black-and-white half-timbering
  people expect is largely a 19th- and 20th-century restoration taste.
- **Windows are small and dark.** Glass was expensive; many openings are
  shuttered or closed with oiled cloth.
- The **town hall** appears as a plain gabled block with an arcaded ground
  floor: it is the merchants' hall of **1370**, the council's seat since
  **1419** — the decoration comes later, as above.
- Convents shown: **Wengen** (Augustinian canons, with a tower), and two friars'
  churches with ridge turrets only, which is the correct convention for
  mendicant orders. Their **positions are approximate**.

## 6. The countryside

- **Strip fields (Gewanne)** in blocks of differing direction, in a
  **three-field rotation** — winter corn, spring corn, fallow. This is the
  strongest single "period" signal in an aerial view and it is well founded.
- **Bleaching greens**: white cloth laid out on the meadows by the water.
  Ulm's wealth was **fustian (Barchent)** — linen warp, cotton weft — sold to
  Genoa, Venice, Geneva, Lyon, the Netherlands and England; **about 60,000
  pieces a year were bleached here in the 15th century**.
- **River traffic**: flat-bottomed barges and a timber raft. Ulm is the head of
  Danube navigation. (The famous *Ulmer Schachtel* as a named type belongs to
  later centuries; the film shows generic late-medieval river craft.)
- Vineyards on south-facing slopes, a wayside chapel, brick and lime kilns
  downwind, and a **gallows** in sight of the roads — the imperial city's right
  of blood justice. These are *typical*, not individually documented here.

## 7. Population and standing

Around 1500 Ulm is at its economic and cultural peak and holds, after
Nuremberg, the **largest imperial-city territory** in what is now Germany. The
city's population at that moment is usually put at roughly **10,000–15,000**
inside the walls (14th-century estimates run 4,000–7,000; the imperial-city
peak of about 21,000 comes around 1600). The film's caption gives the range
rather than a false precision. The **Großer Schwörbrief of 1397** had given the
guilds 30 of the 40 seats on the great council — this is a guild city.

## 8. What is frankly invented

- Every individual house, its plot, its height and its colour.
- The street network's exact geometry.
- The positions of people, carts, animals, boats and birds.
- The weather: a clear late-summer morning with the sun in the east-south-east.
- The music (see below).
- The terrain beyond the immediate valley is shaped to the *right kind* of
  landscape — the Alb edge north and west, the flat plain south — not to a
  survey.

## 9. The music

The score is an **original composition written for this film**, not a
reconstruction of a surviving piece. It is written in the **Dorian mode on D**,
in slow triple metre, for lute, two recorders, a bowed viol drone and bells —
the ordinary furniture of secular music in a German town about 1500. Every
sound is synthesised from first principles (the lute is a Karplus–Strong
plucked string; the bells are inharmonic partial stacks). No historical melody
is quoted.

## 11. Known weaknesses

1. **The 1493 woodcut was not available to trace.** The film reproduces what
   the descriptions of it record, not the image itself. A future pass should
   work directly from the woodcut and from Merian's 1643 engraving.
2. **Monastery and hospital sites are approximate.** They are placed
   plausibly by quarter, not from a cadastral plan.
3. **The choir towers' 1500 height is a guess** within known bounds.
4. **No people are individually modelled** — at this altitude they would be two
   pixels, and false detail is worse than none.
5. **Roof colour is uniform-ish clay red.** Real roofscapes carried more
   variety, including shingle on outbuildings, which the film shows only
   sparingly.
